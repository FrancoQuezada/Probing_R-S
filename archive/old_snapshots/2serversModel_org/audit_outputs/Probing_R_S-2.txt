1     Model description
Simplified Model
Jobs arrive over time and must be routed to one of two back-end servers:

    • Fast server: faster processing, but incurs an additional routing cost.
    • Standard server: slower processing, but no routing cost.

    The fast server is not always the best choice because it may already have a
long queue. Therefore, the routing decision trades off the lower processing time
of the fast server against its routing cost and current congestion.
    Each arriving job has two characteristics:

    • a job value v ∈ [0, 1], which determines the revenue generated by complet-
      ing the job;
    • a hidden binary state indicating whether the job is simple or complex.

    Simple jobs require relatively little processing regardless of the selected
server. In contrast, complex jobs require substantially more processing time,
especially when routed to the standard server. Consequently, complex jobs
benefit the most from being assigned to the fast server.
    The decision maker does not observe whether a job is complex. However, by
probing the job, an imperfect signal can be obtained. This signal is informative,
but not perfectly accurate, and is used to improve the routing decision.
    We consider a family of threshold policies of the form

                       score = av v − aq (qf − qs ) + as s,

where qf and qs denote the current workloads (or queue lengths) of the fast and
standard servers, respectively, and s ∈ {0, 1} is the probing signal. The job is
routed to the fast server whenever

                                     score ≥ τ,

where τ is the policy threshold.

    The likelihood-ratio approach exploits the fact that the underlying random-
ness of the system (arrival process, true job characteristics, service times, etc.)
is independent of the probing decision z. Changing z only modifies the distri-
bution of the probing outcomes and, consequently, the information available to
the routing systems. As a result, the routing decisions and the realized system
trajectory change with z, even though they are driven by the same underlying re-
alization. This separation allows the underlying realizations to be recycled while
accounting for different probing decisions through likelihood-ratio weights.

    [de: New text about the green gradient search.]
    The LR gradient estimator ∇z J(z) derived above can be evaluated at any
z. Hence, we can think of J(z) as a surrogate for the objective function, which
we can then attempt to optimize using a gradient based search. In the setup
we have tested, we perform n initial replications under the no-probing case and
obtain a static surrogate J that we then optimize. We know J will be biased
and (possibly) highly variable for some z, like those z far from 0. As we increase
n, we expect the bias and variable to both vanish, but possibly at a slow rate
for z far from 0.
    We could alternatively take n observations at each iterate z τ (τ = 0, 1, 2, . . . )
visited by the search and use only these n observations to compute a LR gradient
estimate and thereby take a gradient step. The advantage of this approach is
that LR gradient estimator should be more accurate because it is based on repli-
cations under the current z (hence the likelihood ratio in ∇z J(z) is uniformly
1 and therefore vanishes). The downside of this approach is that it essentially
discards all previous simulation replications taken under past probing decisions.
    The idea behind green simulation is to achieve a greater effective sample
size (and therefore a less variable LR gradient estimator) by reuse historical
observations previously collected throughout the search. These observations are
appropriately reweighted by a LR and score function, both of which depend on
the current iterate: the z τ where the gradient is being estimated. [de: Aside: An
early paper of mine on the green gradient algorithm demonstrated that there is
an additional bias introduced by this reuse due to the observations from different
iterates being dependent. This dependence arises because the search trajectory
(and hence the observations you will see next) depends on the observations you
already saw.]

In this setup, we were able to first observe η (from probing) and then decide on
an optimal ACTION i before experiencing a reward Y that depended on further
randomness ξ. In our production example, on the other hand, we have to commit
to a POLICY beforehand when we choose the coefficients and thresholds. This
policy then dictates which action we take when we see a certain probing signal
η. In the production example, i corresponds to the policy parameters, not the
decision to route the job to the standard or fast line. The examples we’ve been
looking at recently do not have the full “two-stage” setup where we pick z,
observe η, pick i, and observe ξ. Instead, we are simultaneously picking z and
i and then experiencing ξ (which now includes η). Put differently, there is no
expectation outside the maximum, hence our formulas for the LR estimators
did not involve the Monte Carlo integration that is present in the more general
two-stage model we started with.

8     Example 4: A possible level II case
8.1    Production and rework system
We consider a production system composed of multiple parallel assembly lines,
a quality-control station, and a centralized rework center. Let C = {1, . . . , m}
denote the set of assembly lines or product families and denote the set of as-
sembly lines, and let [0, H] denote the operating shift. Each assembly line c ∈ C
continuously produces units of a particular product family during an operating
shift.
    After assembly, every completed unit undergoes a quality-control test. A
unit that passes the test is conforming and proceeds directly to packaging and
shipment. A unit that fails the test requires corrective processing and becomes a


                                       15
rework job. These defective units arrive dynamically at the centralized rework
center as production progresses. The line of origin is observed when the job
arrives at the rework center. However, the job may require either simple or
complex rework. Its complexity is uncertain and is influenced probabilistically
by the latent condition of its assembly line of origin.
    The rework center contains two alternative resources:
   • the fast server, representing an expert technician or an advanced repair
     station with shorter service times but a higher operating cost;
   • the standard server, representing a regular repair station with a lower
     operating cost but longer service times, particularly for complex jobs.

    Each assembly line c ∈ C is associated with a known product value vc ∈ [0, 1].
Before the next job arrives, its value is unknown because its line of origin is
unknown. Once the job arrives, however, its line of origin is observed and
its value vc is determined. Therefore, job values are not included as separate
random elements in ξ.
    After service at either server, the repaired unit is released back to the normal
production flow and subsequently proceeds to packaging and shipment. Thus,
conforming units bypass the rework center, while defective units are routed
through one of the two rework resources before reaching the final production
stage.
